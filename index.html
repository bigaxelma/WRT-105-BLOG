How to be yourself on the College Essay

by Axel Ma

I used to never worry about getting into a good college. I thought that as long as I got good grades and did well on the SAT, every college would accept me.

Today, I’m actually applying. And it’s nothing like I thought. Seventeen years of life now has to be compressed into a few essays and a report card, a task that is literally impossible. So, people try to make themselves look as impressive as they can. Every experience has a grand purpose, every activity demonstrates some obnoxiously cliché lesson, and every essay is about the amazing nonprofit work they did.

Ugh.

Is the goal to show colleges my personality, or is it to paint myself as the person they want me to be?

Paul Fain’s article, “Social Class Influences Where Even Valedictorians Go to College, Research Finds,” helped me understand why hard work alone isn’t enough. Fain explains that students don’t always receive the same quality of college guidance, noting that “the top students actually get worse guidance than average ones” (Fain). This challenged my assumption that doing well in school could get you anywhere. A student whose parents understand admissions or who has someone to review their essays has an advantage over someone figuring everything out alone. Both students might be equally deserving, but they don’t start at the same point. And when one student knows how to present themselves while another doesn’t, how much of the difference comes down to merit?

Malcolm Gladwell’s “Getting In” complicates this question further. Gladwell writes, “if a definition of merit based on academic prowess was leading to the wrong kind of student, the solution was to change the definition of merit” (Gladwell). Colleges aren’t just looking for good grades; they’re looking for particular kinds of people. I understand why, but it makes the process confusing. I can work toward a specific SAT score, but how am I supposed to know whether my personality is enough? Should I be more intellectual, creative, or well-rounded? When colleges decide what they value, applicants are left guessing how they’ll be perceived. And when you’re trying to write an essay about yourself, it’s easier to write about the perfect person that can fill out any and all criteria, rather than writing about the person you really are.

James Warren’s “The Rhetoric of College Application Essays: Removing Obstacles for Low Income and Minority Students” explains the difficulty of balancing self appeal, and self identity. Warren argues that college essays “actually function as arguments that make a case for the applicant’s potential as a college student” (44). Writing about myself seemed easy until I actually tried to do it. How could I make an ordinary experience sound meaningful without being corny or obnoxious? Then I realized, I’m seventeen. What do I know about the world?

My experiences don’t need to be extraordinary to say something about me. There is beauty and nuance in simple stories, and in what we take away from them. Rather than write about what seems meaningful to admissions officers, write about what is meaningful to you. What matters to you is what you can write about most passionately and authentically. It’s your voice, your perspective, your essay. The topic doesn’t need to be unique, impressive, or niche. What matters is why the topic is important to you.

Of course, Warren has a point. An essay isn’t just a diary entry, and you still need to communicate your experiences in a way that helps colleges understand your potential and personality. And, as Fain’s article suggests, not everyone has equal access to people who can help them do that. But being persuasive doesn’t have to mean being inauthentic. You can tell a story honestly while still making it clear what it reveals about you.

Colleges ask students to be authentic while also asking them to persuade admissions officers. You’re supposed to be yourself, but somehow also the most interesting version of yourself, all within 650 words. I think that’s where we get it wrong. Don’t try to write a fake version of yourself that fits the college. Write about the real you. In trying to fabricate the perfect applicant, you lose the unique voice that gives the application meaning. You don’t have to invent a person worth accepting. You have to show colleges why the person who is there is worth accepting.
